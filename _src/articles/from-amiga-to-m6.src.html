From Amiga to M6

In 1987 the Amiga 2000 spread its computer across a board of separate chips, and its Bridgeboard added a whole second computer on a card. Apple's M6 puts those same jobs on one piece of silicon. This guide starts with the Amiga board, opens up the M6, and ends with side-by-side comparisons of the M6 and M5 Macs and the iPad Pro.

Commodore Amiga 2000 · Rev 6 motherboard · A2088 Bridgeboard

One job per chip, and a PC on a card

The Amiga 2000 ran a Motorola 68000 CPU alongside a set of custom chips designed by Jay Miner's team, each with one job: memory and DMA, video, sound and disk, glue logic, I/O. Add an A2088 Bridgeboard and the same case held a second computer, a complete IBM PC/XT. Tap a part on the board to see what it did and which part of the M6 does that job today.

The CPU runs at 7.16 MHz on NTSC machines and 7.09 MHz on PAL machines. Earlier boards had 512 KB of chip RAM plus 512 KB of other RAM.

Chip RAM: unified memory in 1987

The CPU and every custom chip shared one pool called chip RAM. Agnus, silkscreened "FAT LADY" on the A2000 board, decided who got each memory cycle. Display fetches came first, then the Blitter, and the 68000 used the cycles that were left. A flag called BLITHOG could even let the Blitter lock the CPU out. The M6 works on the same idea at a much larger scale: its CPU, GPU and Neural Engine share one 16 GB pool through one memory controller.

The A2088 Bridgeboard: a second computer

The A2088 was an IBM PC/XT on one card: an Intel 8088 at 4.77 MHz, 512 KB of its own RAM, an XT-compatible BIOS and a socket for an 8087 math coprocessor. It plugged into a PC (ISA) slot and the Zorro slot lined up with it, so it sat on both computers' buses at once. The A2000 board has two of those lined-up pairs.

The two computers talked through about 128 KB of shared memory, and each could interrupt the other. Commodore's Janus library handled the link. The PC's CGA or monochrome screen appeared in a window on the Amiga desktop, the PC used the Amiga's keyboard and mouse, and the PC side could store files on the Amiga's drives. Later Bridgeboards moved up to a 286 (A2286) and a 386SX (A2386SX).

What came next

The ECS chipset (1990) brought a Super Agnus that could address 2 MB of chip RAM and a Super Denise with sharper productivity screen modes. AGA (1992, in the A1200, A4000 and CD32) replaced Agnus with the 32-bit Alice and Denise with Lisa, with a palette of 16.8 million colors. Paula never changed: the A4000 still used the same four sound channels as the 1985 Amiga 1000.

Apple M6 · 2 nm · announced Aug 25, 2026

Every chip, one die

The M6 is Apple's first 2 nm chip. It debuted in the Mac mini and now also powers the 14-inch MacBook Pro. CPU, GPU, Neural Engine, memory controllers, caches, media and display engines, Thunderbolt and the Secure Enclave all share one die, and the memory sits in the same package. Mac mini M6 · MacBook Pro 14" M6

Same chip, different boards

The Mac mini and the 14-inch MacBook Pro use the same M6 package, so their CPU, GPU, memory, Wi-Fi chip and Thunderbolt ports match. The chip decides what's possible. Each logic board decides which of those features reach a port and adds the few parts the chip doesn't include.

Which to buy

Air M5

Travel companion

Email, web, light coding and remote sessions to a desktop Mac. It's silent, the lightest of the three and about $1,100 on sale. The 60 Hz 500-nit screen and two ports are its limits.

Pro M6

The sweet spot

For $700 more than the Air's list price you get a 120 Hz XDR screen, HDMI and SD, a third port, a fan, 1 TB of storage, the longest battery life and the fastest single core. Engadget gave it 9/10.

Pro M5 Pro

Sustained heavy work

Long 4K or ProRes exports, big builds, local AI that needs 48–64 GB, three displays, Thunderbolt 5 storage. If you're spending this much, the $2,899 OLED version is lighter and has a far better screen, but its fans are louder and its screen flickers.
